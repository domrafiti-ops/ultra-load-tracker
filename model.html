Two-Channel Ultra Load Model

Mountain running loads the body along two axes that do not move together: the oxygen cost of climbing, and the eccentric cost of descending. One number cannot hold both. These are two equations — AeL for aerobic dose, MechL for mechanical dose — with every coefficient traced to a source or flagged as an assumption.

The two equations

Channel 1 · Internal / cardiorespiratory

Aerobic Load — AeL

Dose to the oxygen-transport and metabolic systems. One hour at 50 % heart-rate reserve ≈ 100 AU.

AeL = Σt Δt · w(Ft) · δ(t)
w(F)
0.64·e1.92F (male) · 0.86·e1.67F (female) — Banister TRIMP weighting, fitted to the ΔHR–blood-lactate relation
F
fractional reserve intensity, 0–1
δ(t)
min(1 + 0.035·max(0, (t−90)/60), 1.30) — durability up-weight, t = elapsed minutes

Tier A — heart rate available

Ft = (HRt − HRrest) / (HRmax − HRrest)

Tier B — terrain-derived, no HR needed

V̇O₂t = 60 · (mtot/mbody) · C(it) · vt / 20.9
Ft = V̇O₂t / (V̇O₂maxalt − 3.5)
V̇O₂maxalt = V̇O₂max · (1 − 0.065 · max(0, (h−1500)/1000))

C(i) is the Minetti gradient cost polynomial, running form below +20 % grade and walking form above it. Minetti's costs are net of resting metabolism, so the −3.5 makes F a reserve ratio — which is what licenses feeding it into a weighting fitted to %HRR, since %V̇O₂R ≈ %HRR.

Channel 2 · External / tissue

Mechanical Load — MechL

Dose to muscle, tendon and bone. Scaled so 100 AU = the canonical muscle-damaging downhill protocol.

MechL = 100 · [ (1−ω)·Ê + ω·B̂ ] · κp · κs

Eccentric channel Ê — muscle damage

Ê = ( Σj Nj · ujkm ) / Eref
uj = mtot · g · h⁻j / Nj

Cyclic channel B̂ — bone & tendon

B̂ = ( Σj Nj · [ (vj/vref)1.13 · γ(ij) ]kb ) / Bref
Nj
steps in segment = cadence × duration
h⁻j
descent in segment; climbs contribute 0 to Ê
mtot
body mass + carried mass; g = 9.81
km, kb
damage exponents — 1.5 and 3.0 by default
γ(i)
1.2 descending · 1.0 level · 0.7 climbing
ω
0 = pure muscle view, 1 = pure bone view

Report Ê and B̂ separately whenever you can. Blending them is a convenience, not a physiological claim.

The one rule that keeps this honest

Never add AeL and MechL. They are a coordinate pair, not a total. A 3-hour flat tempo is high-aerobic / low-mechanical. A slow 2000 m descent is low-aerobic / very-high-mechanical. Collapsing them is exactly the error that lets a coach load a runner into a stress fracture while the training-load chart stays green.

The whole-session shortcut

The daily Load Log does not ask for segments, and it does not need to. Both collapses are quantified, and both are asserted in the test suite.

Eccentric — exactly equivalent, not approximate

Ê = 0.303 · D⁻ · (mtot/70)1.5 · √(ī · SL)

At km = 1.5 the segment sum collapses algebraically: Σ N·(mgh/N)1.5 becomes (mg)1.5·D⁻·√(ī·SL). Descent metres enter linearly; mean grade and step length only under a square root, so a 20 % error in either moves Ê by 10 %.

ī is derived from what a watch already gives you: distance is split between climbing and descending in proportion to D+ and D−. On the worked example that recovers 0.160 against a true weighted 0.169 — a 5 % grade error, so a 2.1 % load error.

The number to carry in your head

At 70 kg on 12 % mean descent grade: ≈ 10.8 AU per 100 m of descent. At 20 %: 13.9. At 30 %: 17.0. You can price a route off the elevation profile before you leave the house.

Aerobic — no approximation worth worrying about

AeL = w(F̄) · I(T)

I(T) is the durability integral ∫δ(t)dt in closed form, so exactness is free:

T ≤ 90 → I = T
90 < T ≤ 604.3 → I = T + 0.00029167·(T−90)²
T > 604.3 → I = 681.45 + 1.30·(T−604.3)

Using one average through a convex weighting under-counts by roughly 1.843·σ²F. Continuous mountain running sits at σF ≈ 0.05–0.12, a 0.5–2.7 % under-count. Interval sessions reach σF ≈ 0.20 and a 7 % under-count — log the intervals and the rest as two entries.

B̂ is the one casualty

Its effective speed exponent is 3.39. Averaging a 4 km/h hike with an 11 km/h descent under-counts by more than half. The Log shows a dash unless you supply climb and descent minutes — a fabricated B̂ is worse than no B̂.

Why gradient has to be in the equation

The metabolic cost of covering a metre is not linear in grade, and it is not symmetric. Descending gets cheaper down to about −20 %, then expensive again — while the mechanical cost climbs monotonically the whole way down. Any model that treats a kilometre as a kilometre is wrong by a factor of five at the ends of this curve.

Running Cr(i) Walking Cw(i) Level running reference, 3.6 J·kg⁻¹·m⁻¹

Minetti et al. (2002). Running: Cr = 155.4i⁵ − 30.4i⁴ − 43.3i³ + 46.3i² + 19.5i + 3.6. Walking: Cw = 280.5i⁵ − 58.7i⁴ − 76.8i³ + 51.9i² + 19.6i + 2.5. Above roughly +25 % grade the walking curve sits below the running curve — the physiological reason mountain athletes hike steep climbs.

Derivation notes

The durability term δ(t)

This makes the equation ultra-specific, and it is the piece I am least willing to defend to three decimals.

Threshold intensities migrate downward during long efforts. Hunter's London Marathon cohort lost 5.5 % of speed at lactate threshold after 90 minutes. Hamilton's cyclists lost 6 % of power at VT1 after 150 minutes. Both mean the same thing: at hour four, a given heart rate represents a higher fraction of your current capacity than it did at minute ten. Flat TRIMP integration silently under-prices the back half of every long day.

Those imply 3.7 %/h and 2.4 %/h. I took 3.5 %/h with onset at 90 minutes, because both protocols were ≥90 min. The 1.30 cap is an assumption — decay almost certainly plateaus, but nothing quantifies where. It binds at ~10 hours and adds ~12.6 % to a 10-hour effort versus naïve TRIMP.

What I deliberately left out

No heat multiplier in Tier A. Heat raises heart rate directly — Rosbrook found +23 bpm of drift over 90 min at 37 °C versus +10 at 20 °C. Integrating measured HR already captures it; a heat coefficient would double-count.

No altitude multiplier either — the altitude term shrinks V̇O₂max in the denominator instead, which is mechanistically what happens.

Start from negative work, not impact

The obvious move is accelerometry — impact g, GRF, "player load". Matijevich is the reason not to. Across speeds and slopes, GRF metrics were not correlated with tibial compression: impact peak versus peak tibial load came out at r = −0.29 ± 0.37, and 76 of 80 subject-specific correlations showed higher GRF did not mean higher bone load. Wearable "impact load" scores measure the wrong thing.

So: descending ΔH metres, the body absorbs m·g·ΔH joules of negative work — 9.81 J per kg per metre, no fitting required. Minetti's finding that efficiency below −15 % grade approaches that of pure eccentric contraction licenses treating descent as the eccentric channel.

Damage then accumulates as cycles × magnitudek, the structure of a bone S–N curve. Assumption km = 1.5 is not measured — no published dose–response exponent for eccentric running damage exists that I found. It sits above 1 (superlinear, as all fatigue processes are) and well below the 4–12 of cortical-bone strain-life curves.

Adaptation is a response, not a dose

The repeated-bout effect is real and large, and it is tempting to bolt an RBE discount onto MechL. Don't. A 1500 m descent delivers the same mechanical stimulus whether or not you are adapted; what changes is your damage response. Keep MechL as pure dose and apply ρRBE ∈ [0.7, 1.0] on the tolerance side. Bontemps sharpens this: ten downhill sessions reduced soreness but did not change central or peripheral fatigue response — "adapted" is not one number.

Calibration & validation

Where 100 AU comes from

Bontemps' review characterised the literature's canonical muscle-damaging bout by its medians: −12 % grade, 40 minutes, 11.3 km/h. For a 70 kg athlete that is 7533 m of distance, 904 m of descent, 6800 steps at 170 spm, 621 kJ of negative work, 91.29 J per step. Defining it as Ê = 100 AU makes every number a multiple of a protocol the literature agrees causes measurable damage. Bref is a flat 10 km at 12 km/h — 8750 steps — likewise B̂ = 100 AU.

That last row is the validation that matters. Millet's cohort finished with knee-extensor MVC down 35 %, creatine kinase from 144 to 13 633 UI·L⁻¹, and 16 days to baseline. The model prices it at ten canonical damaging protocols back to back.

It also produces a non-obvious insight: per-step load in the race (82.4 J) is lower than in the reference protocol (91.3 J). Ultra runners descend with short, soft, high-cadence steps. Their damage comes almost entirely from cycle count, not per-cycle severity — a real argument for treating descent volume and descent intensity as separate training variables.

What is still missing

Neither equation prices sleep loss, GI failure, thermal strain in Tier A, or the fact that fatigue itself changes mechanics mid-session — Khassetarash showed leg stiffness dropping and duty factor rising through a single 30-minute descent, and Trama found vastus lateralis vibration damping altered after races from 40 to 171 km. A step late in a race is not the same step as one early in it, and no term here captures that.

Acute:chronic workload ratios are also deliberately absent. Wang's review documents the structural problems — ratios of correlated quantities, an initial-load artefact in the EWMA formulation, and inapplicability to sports that taper. Ultra training is nothing but tapers and spikes. Plot the two channels as rolling series and read the shape; do not compute a ratio and trust a threshold.
